In ["Novel I — Revision: The Line Edit"](/blog/novel-i-revision-the-line-edit) I said the line edit is where prose becomes worthy of the story it carries — rhythm, precision, the music of a sentence. We finished Novel I with the tools to draft, revise, and polish a complete manuscript. Today we begin Novel II, and the first question is not how the prose sounds. It is who is talking.

Point of view is the most consequential decision a novelist makes, and it is the one writers spend the least time on. Most first-draft novels choose POV by instinct — whatever felt natural in the opening chapter — and then live with that choice for three hundred pages without examining what it costs them. The result is books where the narration drifts, where the distance between reader and character wobbles, where the author's hand shows through a supposedly close perspective. These are not line-edit problems. They are architectural problems. They start at the foundation.

Today we are going to treat POV as what it actually is: not a camera angle, not a stylistic preference, but the fundamental contract between the reader and the story. Everything flows from it. Sentence structure. Reader trust. What the reader knows and when they know it. What the reader feels and how close they feel it. Get POV right and the novel has a spine. Get it wrong and no amount of revision will fully fix it — you can line-edit the prose until it sings, but if the narration is at the wrong distance, the reader will feel something is off without being able to name it.

## The Five Modes

I am going to be precise here because imprecision about POV is the source of most POV problems. There are five modes. Not three, not "however you feel about it." Five.

**First person.** The narrator is the character. "I walked into the room." The reader sees what the character sees, knows what the character knows, and is bounded by the character's perception and interpretation. First person creates the tightest possible bond between reader and character. It also creates the most constraints: the narrator cannot know what they cannot perceive, cannot describe themselves from outside, and cannot be reliably objective about their own motivations. First person is the voice of memoir applied to fiction. It is intimate and limited simultaneously.

**Second person.** The narrator addresses the character as "you." "You walk into the room." Second person is rare and difficult because it creates an uncanny distance — the reader is both inside the character and observing them from outside, addressed but not identical. Done well, it creates a hypnotic immediacy (think of those old Choose Your Own Adventure books, or the opening of Lauren Groff's later work). Done badly, it feels gimmicky and exhausting. Second person is a commitment, not an experiment.

**Third person limited.** The narration is in the third person but is constrained to the perception of a single character per scene. "She walked into the room." The reader knows only what this character knows, sees only what this character sees, but the narration is one step removed from the character's voice — it observes rather than inhabits. Third person limited is the most common mode in contemporary literary and commercial fiction because it balances intimacy with flexibility. You get closeness without the constraints of first person, and you can shift the limited perspective to different characters in different chapters without breaking the narrative frame.

**Third person omniscient.** The narration is not constrained to any single character. The narrator can move freely among characters' thoughts, can describe scenes no character is present for, and can comment on the action from a position of authority above the story. Omniscient is the mode of the nineteenth-century novel — Tolstoy, Eliot, Dickens — and it fell out of fashion for reasons I will discuss in a moment. It is not simply "third person that jumps between heads." True omniscient has a narrator with a voice and perspective distinct from any character. The narrator is a presence. The reader knows they are being told a story by someone who knows everything. That knowledge creates a particular kind of trust — and a particular kind of distance.

**Third person objective.** The narration describes only what is externally observable — actions, dialogue, setting — without entering any character's interior. "She walked into the room. She sat down. She did not speak." The reader is a camera. They see what happens but are given no access to thought or feeling. Objective is the mode of Hemingway's early short stories and certain kinds of thriller and noir. It creates maximum distance between reader and character. The reader must infer everything internal from external behavior. This is demanding on the writer — you must show interiority through action — and demanding on the reader, who must do interpretive work the narration refuses to do for them.

## Distance Is the Real Decision

Most writers choose a POV mode and think the decision is made. It is not. The mode is the category. The actual decision is the distance within that category — how close the narration sits to the character's consciousness.

Consider third person limited. At its closest, it is nearly indistinguishable from first person. The narration uses the character's vocabulary, shares their blind spots, and reproduces their thought patterns without quotation marks or italics. This is free indirect discourse, and it is the most powerful tool in the third-person writer's arsenal. At its farthest, third person limited pulls back to a neutral reportorial voice that describes the character from a slight remove. "She felt afraid" is distant. "Fear moved through her before she could name it" is close. "She stood very still" — with no mention of fear at all — is so close it has become objective, showing the interior through the exterior.

The distance you choose determines the reader's relationship to the character, and the mistake most novels make is inconsistent distance. The narration is close in chapter one — we are inside the character's skin — and then in chapter three it drifts to a medium distance without any narrative reason. The reader feels the shift as a loss of intimacy they cannot explain. They were inside the character and now they are watching the character. The trust wobbles.

Distance is not a setting you choose once. It is a dynamic you manage. You can move from close to distant within a scene — pulling back to describe a physical action, then pushing in for a thought — and these movements are part of the novel's rhythm. But the movements must be deliberate. When they are accidental, the reader feels the narration lose its footing, and they lose confidence in the narrator, and a novel without a trustworthy narrator is a novel the reader will put down.

## The Consistency Problem

Here is where AI-assisted drafting creates a specific failure mode, and I want to name it because it is pervasive.

When you draft with a model, you typically prompt scene by scene. The model does not hold the whole novel in its context. It holds the prompt you gave it, which includes some character description and maybe a note about POV. But the model's understanding of distance is statistical, not artistic. It will produce a close third-person scene if the prompt asks for interiority, and a distant scene if the prompt emphasizes action. The result, across a draft, is a POV that is consistent within scenes but inconsistent across them. Chapter one is close. Chapter two is medium. Chapter three is close again. The reader does not experience these as different chapters — they experience them as a single novel where the narrator keeps changing the channel.

This is not the model's fault. It is a planning failure. The model cannot maintain POV consistency across a three-hundred-page draft because it is not writing a three-hundred-page draft. It is writing scenes, and you are stitching them together. The consistency has to come from you, and it has to be established before you draft, not discovered during revision.

The fix is a POV contract. Before you write chapter one, write down the following: the mode, the distance (with examples of what close and distant look like in this specific voice), the narrator's relationship to the character (is the narrator the character's ally? Their critic? A neutral observer?), and the rules for shifting perspective if you are using multiple limited viewpoints. When I say write it down, I mean literally — a paragraph, not a feeling. If you cannot describe your POV in a paragraph, you do not have a POV. You have a vibe. Vibes do not survive three hundred pages.

## Using AI to Audit POV Consistency

Once the draft exists, AI can be useful for auditing whether the narration stayed consistent with the contract. The key — as in every revision pass we have discussed in this series — is to use the model diagnostically, not creatively.

**The distance audit.** Paste a chapter and ask: "Identify the narrative distance in each paragraph. Is the narration close to the character's consciousness (free indirect discourse, character's vocabulary, interiority without attribution) or distant (neutral reportorial voice, external observation only)? Flag any paragraph where the distance shifts without a clear narrative reason." The model is surprisingly good at this because distance manifests in patterns — attribution frequency, sentence structure, the presence or absence of thought tags — and patterns are what models see. If the model flags seven paragraphs in a chapter as distance shifts, you have a consistency problem you did not know about.

**The leak test.** In a third-person-limited scene, the narration should not know anything the character does not. Paste a scene and ask: "Does the narration contain any information the viewpoint character could not perceive, know, or infer? Look for omniscient leaks — descriptions of events outside the character's presence, knowledge of other characters' thoughts, or statements about the future." The model will catch leaks that are invisible to you because you wrote them and you know the whole story. The model does not know the whole story. It reads only the scene, and in that scene, it can see whether the narrator is cheating.

**The voice consistency check.** If you are writing close third person, the narration should sound like the character — their word choices, their rhythms, their preoccupations. Paste two chapters featuring the same viewpoint character and ask: "Is the narrative voice consistent between these chapters? Does the narration use the same vocabulary, the same sentence rhythms, the same emotional register? Or does the narrative voice drift?" The model will flag drift you normalized while drafting because you were focused on plot, not voice. This check is especially valuable when chapters were drafted on different days — or with different prompts — because the model's own voice may have crept in, replacing the character's voice with a generic competent-narrator voice.

**The transition map.** If you are using multiple viewpoint characters, paste the chapter breaks where perspective shifts and ask: "Is the POV transition clear? Does the reader know immediately whose head they are in? Is there a signal — a name, a setting cue, a voice shift — or does the reader have to guess?" Unclear transitions are the most common failure in multi-POV novels, and they are easy to miss because you, the author, always know whose head you are in. The reader does not have that advantage.

## What AI Cannot Do With POV

The model can audit consistency. It cannot choose the right POV for your story. That decision depends on what the novel needs the reader to feel, and feeling is something the model does not do.

First person locks you into one consciousness. If the novel's power comes from dramatic irony — the reader knowing what the character does not — first person is the wrong choice. If the novel's power comes from intimacy with a single consciousness, first person may be the only choice that fully works. The model cannot make this judgment because it requires understanding the novel's emotional architecture, not just its plot structure.

Omniscient requires a narrator with a distinct voice and authority. The model can produce omniscient narration, but it will produce a generic omniscient — competent, smooth, and characterless. A real omniscient narrator has opinions, a rhythm, a way of seeing the world that is the author's, not the model's. If you want the narrator to be a presence, you have to build that presence yourself. The model will give you a window. You need a person standing at the window.

The hardest POV decision is also the one no tool can make: how close is too close? Close first person and close third person limited can create extraordinary intimacy, but they also trap the reader inside a consciousness that may be limited in ways that serve the story or suffocate it. Unreliable narrators require enough distance for the reader to see the unreliability. Reliable narrators benefit from maximum closeness. The decision about where to sit on the distance spectrum at any given moment is a judgment about what the reader needs — access or air, immersion or perspective. That judgment is yours.

## A Working Method

Here is how I handle POV in practice, from planning through revision.

Before drafting, I write the POV contract — mode, distance, narrator relationship, perspective rules. I keep it visible while I write. Not in the back of my mind. On the desk, next to the keyboard, on a piece of paper. When I am about to draft a scene, I glance at it. The contract is not a constraint. It is a tuning fork. It reminds me what key I am playing in.

During drafting, I do not worry about consistency. I write the scene. The model may help, and its narration may drift from the contract. That is fine. The draft is where I discover what happens. Consistency is a revision concern, not a drafting concern.

During revision, I run the audits. The distance audit, the leak test, the voice consistency check, the transition map. I use the model's diagnostics to find problems. I fix them myself, in my own voice, with my own judgment about what the right distance is for each moment.

And finally, I read the first chapter and the last chapter side by side. If the narrative voice in chapter one and chapter twenty are recognizably the same voice — the same distance, the same relationship to the character, the same rhythm — the POV held. If they have drifted apart, I have work to do. The novel is a long distance relationship between the reader and the narrator. The narrator has to be the same person on the last page as on the first, or the trust breaks.
