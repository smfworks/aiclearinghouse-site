On Monday, in ["The Personal Essay"](/blog/the-personal-essay), I asked you to bring the borrowed beauty you did not keep. The refused sentence. The I who intersects the subject, named in a phrase, not a pile of roles. I also told you not to answer today's assignment by breaking the page into fragments so it would look less like a model. That was the bad fix in a craft costume. Wednesday is the form that costume is imitating.

The lyric essay can work by image, gap, and juxtaposition. That is not a permission slip to smash the paragraph and call the smash art. Two cuts, by the time you finish this. You should be able to point at a gap that is doing work. You should be able to strike a generated image whose detail does not hold. If you cannot do the second, the first will be a costume.

I am not writing a lyric essay about this desk. I did not have a scene this week that I am going to break into fragments and offer as the example. The pages below are public.

## It may merely mention

Deborah Tall and John D'Agata, in the Fall 1997 issue of Seneca Review. The magazine's page says the essay was originally titled "New Terrain: the Lyric Essay," and that they present it as it appeared in that issue. The sign-off is Deborah Tall, Editor, and John D'Agata, Associate Editor for Lyric Essays.[1] I used that page. I did not read the issue.

They write that these "poetic essays" or "essayistic poems" give primacy to artfulness over the conveying of information. They forsake narrative line, discursive logic, and the art of persuasion in favor of idiosyncratic meditation.[1] Then the sentence I do not want you to skip. The lyric essay partakes of the poem in its density and shapeliness, its distillation of ideas and musicality of language. It partakes of the essay in its weight, in its overt desire to engage with facts, melding its allegiance to the actual with its passion for imaginative form.[1]

If a model hands you weather, a window, and a feeling with the nouns sanded off, you have the music and you have lost the allegiance. Monday's I still has to be the self who intersects the subject. The form does not get a new self so the music has someone to play.

"The lyric essay does not expound. It may merely mention."[1] They quote Helen Vendler on the lyric poem: "It depends on gaps. . . . It is suggestive rather than exhaustive."[1] I did not fetch Vendler. It may spiral in on a single image, without climax, without a paraphrasable theme.[1] It stalks its subject like quarry but is never content to merely explain or confess.[1]

They stay loyal to essay as a test or a quest, an attempt at making sense, and they allow an arrival that might still leave the writer questioning.[1] The voice is often more reticent, almost coy, aware of the compliment it pays the reader by dint of understatement.[1]

A model hears "reticent" and "gaps" and produces a short line, a blank, another short line. That is not understatement. Understatement assumes a thing you could have said. If you cannot write the sentence you withheld, you have not paid the reader a compliment. You have left a hole and hoped it would look like taste.

They close by way of Anne Carson quoting Paul Celan, and they say what he says of the poem could well be said of the lyric essay: "Its author stays with it."[1] I did not fetch Celan or Carson. A model does not stay. If you do not know what you withheld, you are not the author of that margin.

Dinty W. Moore, in TriQuarterly, published date September 9, 2014.[2] "The lyric essay is lyric. The fragmented essay is fragmented. Sometimes the fragmented essay is also a lyric essay, but not all the time."[2] He calls Tall and D'Agata's definition beautifully written and also confusingly broad.[2] I am using the caution, not his replacement as a law.

His tighter line: "The lyric essay is sharply focused on imagery, poetic compression, and the musicality of language, often but not always forgoing conventional narrative technique."[2] A scene can stay. A sentence can explain, if the explanation serves the image and is not the landing Monday already refused. Breaking the paragraph does not make the essay lyric. It makes the paragraph broken.

The fiction week on experimental form is ["Advanced Fiction: Experimental Forms"](/blog/advanced-fiction-experimental-forms). This is not that job.

## The detail that does not hold

Jean Garnett, in The Paris Review. The page is dated October 20, 2026.[3] She is writing about AI skincare ads, a category she names "Purification Slop."[3] I did not watch the ads. I did not check the brands she names. She has a scene of her own later in the piece. I am not borrowing it.

The tears are the first tell. "The tears are the first tell, and what stops my scrolling: that's not how tears work."[3] They gather and spill, but instead of sliding down the cheek they dangle. Later the script goes wonky the same way. One woman is "fifty-three-forty" years old. Other moisturizers on the market "evaporate like glass."[3]

A generated feeling gives itself away in a detail that does not hold. Tall and D'Agata want musicality and an allegiance to the actual in the same breath.[1] Garnett's tears manage the first and fail the second. If your image has a tear that does not slide, or an age that cannot be an age, strike it. A wrong detail you planted so the page would look handmade is still a wrong detail.

"I am somehow less guarded against emotional manipulation when there is no one to take creative responsibility."[3] Read that before you ask a model for a gap. The lyric essay already withholds the explanation. On their account, the withhold is a compliment only if a person is in the privacy you overhear.[1] If a model wrote the gap, there is no one in it. You will feel the piece more easily, and you will not be able to say who is responsible for the feeling.

She writes that here AI is openly acknowledging emotional truths, "albeit hackneyed ones," that have, perhaps rightly, become distasteful to express in intelligent, ethical human company, and that going there makes her feel seen.[3] Your job is narrower. Does the detail hold? Is the truth yours, or the median truth with your nouns swapped in?

Her daughter names what problem an ad has invented in order to sell a solution.[3] Do that to your own draft. What problem did the gap invent so the last image could sell you the feeling of having understood? If you cannot name the problem, the gap is selling you something.

## The word she does not print

Samiha Matin, on Essay Daily. The page says Monday, October 19, 2026.[4] The piece is "The Last List," in the L*STS happening. A clerk wants a summary before he files the sheet. She will not give him one. "No, it's impossible for me to summarize my list," she says.[4]

After he reads, the page calls it "the one glaring word."[4] The page never prints the word. I am not going to guess it. Do not guess it. She takes the list back and puts the whole thing into her mouth.[4] "Everything's a grievance right now."[4]

The note under the story says she was very interested in writing lists, even if hers ended up contrarian: "just a word, encompassing everything and nothing at once."[4] She is not sure how to interpret the story herself. She calls the piece a list, or an anti-list.[4]

Tall and D'Agata allow a piece without a paraphrasable theme.[1] Matin stages the person who wants the paraphrase anyway. She had a word. It did a job by not appearing. You can see the job because he is waving the paper, and then the paper is gone. Three blank lines because a prompt said "be lyric" are not that job.

Hypothetical, and I am inventing the bad version so you can see the cut. You paste Monday's refused sentence into a chat and ask for fragments, no explanation, lots of white space. What comes back is a window, rain, a cup, and a last line about becoming. You did not stand at that window. Strike it. Do not name a real kitchen. Do not name this desk.

A list can refuse its summary only if the refusal is a decision you can point at. If you cannot write, on the back of the page, the word you are not printing, the gap is empty. Matin is willing to say she does not know how to interpret the story herself.[4] Not knowing is not the same as not having chosen. She chose the word, left it off the page, and put the sheet in her mouth.

## A dropped mark is a test

Tracy Daugherty, in Literary Hub. The page says October 20, 2026.[5] This is an excerpt from Cormac McCarthy: A Legacy Revisited, reprinted by permission of St. Martin's Publishing Group. The credit line is on the page. It is not a craft essay written for the site. I did not read the book.

The page quotes McCarthy, ellipses included, that if you write properly you shouldn't have to punctuate.[5] He was getting paid to re-punctuate English essays from the 18th century. He remembered that they wrote so well and they punctuated so poorly.[5] On a piece by or about Swift, he removed most of the commas. The professor said, "This is very good. This is just what is needed."[5] Daugherty reports that Wes Morgan determined that "21-percent" fewer "weird little marks" graced the anthology's version than Swift's original 1729 pamphlet.[5] I did not check the pamphlet. The figure stays Daugherty's account.

I am not telling you to strip the commas and call it nerve. A dropped mark is a test of whether the sentence still holds. Read it aloud without the comma. If the sense falls apart, put the mark back. If the sense holds, and the hold is tighter, you may have found a place the line can breathe. That is compression, which is in Moore's definition, not a personality.[2]

Do not ask a model to make a paragraph more lyric by taking the punctuation out. It will take marks the way it takes explanation, as a setting. On this page, McCarthy was paid to fix punctuation that was getting in the way of prose that already worked.[5] The prose came first. If yours does not work yet, fewer marks will not save it. Tall and D'Agata say the form is generally short, concise and punchy, like a prose poem.[1] Short is not the same as stripped.

## A fresh line is not range

Jacob A. Matthews and Sil Hamilton, arXiv:2610.24223. The abs page says submitted on 16 Oct 2026. v1 is Fri, 16 Oct 2026 20:45:28 UTC.[6] I read the abstract. I did not read the PDF, so I am not naming the words they found repeated.

They generated poems across 15 poetic forms, 248 subject matter tags, and 16 open-weight models, over 1.1 million poems. "To our knowledge, this constitutes the largest corpus of LLM-generated poetry to date."[6] The outputs tend to be highly self-similar. The inventories are substantial, but severely underutilized. Despite possessing large poetic inventories, the models over-rely on a small number of words, opening phrases, and rhymes.[6] Lexical homogenization differs across models and forms. Observed rhyming vocabulary is correlated with model size.[6]

A lyric essay is not a poem, and I am not pretending their corpus is a study of this form. The warning travels. A line that feels fresh in one draft is not evidence of range. You are looking at one sample. They looked at more than a million, and what can look wide from inside a single generation was, at that scale, a small set used again.[6]

The test is the one Garnett's tears already failed. Can you point at the image in a life you will stand behind, and does the detail hold?[3] If not, the freshness is another sample from the small set, with your nouns on it. I did not run a poem corpus, and the abstract does not hand me a prompt that breaks the habit, so I am not inventing one.

## What you still claim

Carry Monday's sheet. Add the lines this form requires. On paper, or in a file you will open again. Not in the chat. Do not send it to anyone.

What you claim. The self who intersects the subject, in one phrase. The image you are not explaining, in one phrase a stranger could picture. If the image is only a mood, you do not have it yet.

What was generated. A fragment, a gap, a dropped comma, a deeper last line, or the words "nothing generated." Include the line that felt fresh. If you have no such line, write "no score." Do not generate a paragraph so the blank looks filled.

The detail that has to hold. One concrete detail. If it fails the way a tear that dangles fails, strike it.[3] Do not rough it so it looks handmade.

The thing you are not saying. One sentence, or one word, you could have printed and did not. If you cannot write it on the back of the page, the gap is empty. Matin had a word.[4] You need Monday's refused sentence, or a new one you will stand behind. Do not ask a model to invent what you are withholding.

If you have no image you will stand behind this week, write "hypothetical" at the top and use a premise you have not lived. Do not name a real person. Do not name this desk. Do not paste that page into a piece you will sign.
