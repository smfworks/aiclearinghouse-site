In ["Genre: Horror"](/blog/genre-horror) I said the prior there is an explanation that feels like an ending. Literary fiction has the cousin of that move. The cheap definition is a sensitive person plus an epiphany. The epiphany is what the model is for. Ask for literary and the furniture arrives already interpreted: a window in late light, a last paragraph that announces what the walk meant. Completeness of insight is a brochure with the lights on. A brochure is not a novel.

On Wednesday the floor was a threat that does not refund when the lights come on. A revelation that did not cost is the cousin of a threat that got explained, and personal is not the same as happened. Friday's prior is prouder of the opposite kindness. Wisdom feels like depth. The prior is built to finish.

By the end you should be able to account for the door, keep a secret inside the sentence, and tell a page that shows promise from a packet you can point at a magazine. Stop asking a second model whether the first model's wise sentence is the literary one.

## The sentence that arrives on schedule

In ["Genre Calibration: Training AI for Fiction vs Non-Fiction"](/blog/genre-calibration-training-ai-for-fiction-vs-non-fiction) I said the model has priors for genre furniture. Literary furniture is a sentence that begins "She understood, then," or "Only later would he realize." That is not observation. The paragraph got ready to be wise, and the model obliged.

Fluency goes to the bad pile, the way horror fluency went to fog and romance fluency, in ["Genre — Romance, Horror, Literary: Romance"](/blog/genre-romance), went to heat. A beloved author's name in the prompt gets you cadence. Cadence can sit on a fact. It cannot replace one. In ["Novel II — Deep Craft: Showing vs Telling"](/blog/novel-ii-deep-craft-showing-vs-telling) telling does the reader's work. Here it refunds the scene. The walk happened. The understanding arrives because an open question looked unfinished. You hired a life and received a moral.

I don't trust a paragraph that becomes wise on schedule. If the insight was not already paid for by something specific on the page, the last sentence is doing the work the scene refused.

Here is the kind of line the prior likes. Hypothetical, not a logged generation, and not a sentence from anyone's book: "She understood, then, that the closed door had only ever been her reluctance to be known." That is Wednesday's refund in a cleaner shirt. The door was a door. Reluctance is the caption. Strike it. If nothing is left, the paragraph was the caption.

Before you generate, write the sentence the epiphany is not allowed to say. Want, method, cost, the same lines as Wednesday, pointed at insight instead of threat. Then read the last sentence of each paragraph you actually produce. If it interprets the paragraph, cut it. If the paragraph dies without that sentence, you had a gloss waiting for a story to justify it. I am naming a prior so you can strike it. I am not claiming a run from this desk.

## The door is yours

Amanda Mortlock, in a Writer's Digest essay on novels versus television, draws the line a model will sand off. "The biggest difference between writing TV and novels: In a novel, everything is your problem. Anything you want the reader to visualize or understand, you have to account for. Where are they, what do they look like, what are they wearing, where exactly is the door?"[2] On a show, she writes, there are whole departments for each of those questions. Get too specific and the thing you described may not exist, and building it costs the production money.

Her example of the sanding is a house, and I am quoting her example, not borrowing her novel. The novel line is "a Victorian monstrosity from the nineteenth century, a shambolic Queen Anne disrupting the tidy procession of tasteful neighboring Colonials," with peeling paint "once a merry shade of Pepto-Bismol" that "has since mellowed out into a dirty cotton-candy pink." That gets distilled to "a run-down historical house."[2] The short version can be handed to a department. The pink cannot. If your page can survive being boiled down to the script note, you published the note.

She puts numbers on the difference. Her last episode had just under 9,000 words for an hour. The novel is around 105,000.[2] A script, she writes, "is a sales tool and a blue print for the final product." She spells blueprint as two words, and I am leaving it. "A novel is the whole thing. It has to capture the visuals and sounds and performances of the world and story and characters without having any of those elements to lean on."[2]

Literary fiction is that problem with the departments gone. Ask a model to make a passage "more literary" and the prior offers the distillation in a better coat: a house that seems to hold its breath. I am describing the move, not logging a run. Restraint that loses the paint is a script note that learned a cadence.

Account for the door yourself before you ask anyone to elevate the prose. Where it is. What it looks like. What it costs to use. If you cannot answer those without generating, you do not have a scene yet.

Take one paragraph you already have. This part does not need a model. Underline every noun a camera would have to build. If the only nouns are light, silence, and years, you have weather. Put one shootable noun back, and give it a cost. Hypothetical, so you can see the difference, not a scene I am attributing to a person: the cup is chipped, and she turns it so the chip faces the wall. That is not an epiphany. It is accounting. The model will offer to explain the chip. That explanation is Wednesday's wiring again. Decline it.

## The secret stays in the sentence

Mortlock's other line is the one this genre lives on. "If a character has a secret, the prose could tell the reader what that is without any of the other characters finding out."[2] A script has to see the secret or hear it. Dialogue, something written down, a flashback.[2] The novel can know a thing the room does not.

The prior treats that gap as unfinished, the way an unexplained threat looked unfinished on Wednesday, so it speaks the secret. She understood. He finally said the thing about his father. The last page puts the reader, the character, and the theme in one meeting. Clearer sentences are the robbery, same as they were when horror explained the switch.

You do not have to ban interiority. Mortlock's point is that prose is allowed to hold what the room cannot hear. The failure is the sentence that spends the secret so nobody has to carry it. Write the secret in a line only the reader can use. Then delete the line that explains why it matters. If the scene still stands, the secret was working. If it collapses, the secret was a caption.

After a draft, mark every sentence in which a character realizes, understands, finally sees, or would only later know. Keep one, if the cost is already on the page and the sentence does not translate the cost into a theme. Cut the rest. Do not ask the model whether the secret is landing. In this prior, landing means announced.

## A voice you can attribute

On a show she did not create, Mortlock's goal "is for people who read the script to not know it was me."[2] "The voice of the novel is still the author's."[2] She has collaborators. The voice does not become a room. Literary fiction fails the television test on purpose. If a careful reader cannot tell the page was yours, you wrote the show.

A circle of models will agree with you, and the agreement is not a second opinion.

Ben Wigler and Maria Tsfasman, in a paper submitted 29 September 2026 and published in the proceedings of the Third Conference on Language Modeling, name the false plurality a writer can mistake for notes. I read the abstract, not the PDF. This is their claim about a measurement, not a procedure I can hand you, and I am not turning it into a genre rule. "Open-ended LLM homogeneity can create false plurality when several systems appear to offer independent perspectives while returning the same familiar default."[3] On a prompt bank they call Infinity-Chat 100, their CHOIR measure "reproduces high surface agreement (93/100 prompts above chance) while separating narrow prompts from broad prompts with recoverable depth."[3] They also write that base-model identity "remains the strongest recoverable signature, and persona prompts shift surfaced concepts within base-model signatures."[3]

Three models agreeing on the literary move is not three editors. A persona prompt that says precise, withholding, literary does not walk you out of the base model's signature. It rearranges the furniture inside it. In ["The Multi-Voice Workshop — Switching Personas Mid-Project"](/blog/the-multi-voice-workshop-switching-personas-mid-project) I treated a persona as a tool you choose. Their abstract is a correction aimed at ensembles, not a law of sentences. 93 of 100 is their result on their bank, under their measure. Agreement is cheap, and it will feel like a workshop if you wanted one.

In ["Style Transfer: Teaching AI Your Voice"](/blog/style-transfer-teaching-ai-your-voice) the risk was the model wearing you. Here the risk is you wearing the model and calling it style because a second model nodded. In ["Authenticity — When Is It \"Your\" Work?"](/blog/authenticity-when-is-it-your-work) the question was attribution. Mortlock already answered the practical version. On the show, invisibility is the job. In the novel, invisibility is the loss. If you cannot tell your paragraph from the model's without the label, you do not have a voice yet. Sit with the door until the sentence could only have been written by someone who had to account for the paint.

## Promise is a reason to ask for more

Owen Park, assistant editor at The Paris Review, opened the magazine's October submissions note on 1 October 2026 by refusing the credential test. "We know that the next writer we fall for won't necessarily have an M.F.A. or an agent, and so the slush pile remains our best line of contact with the emerging authors whose names we haven't yet heard."[1] This is not a story about this desk. I am not inventing a submission. I am using his account of how one magazine treats a page that shows promise, because a model will offer you a page that looks like one.

He says a strong piece of prose is sometimes taken straight from Submittable. "More often, however, when we read something we think shows real promise, we'll reach out to the writer, sharing thoughts on what they've sent us and asking to see more."[1] That is how Brian Ma's "Their Utopia" reached the Fall issue. Ma had already sent several stories. A reader on their team, January Santoso, urged the latest last October. They got in touch, asked what else he had, and a novel in progress arrived, about a haunting series of events at a cell-phone factory in Vietnam, from which the story is extracted.[1]

Chad Fore's "Lil Spooky," in the Summer issue, took a longer correspondence. It began in early 2025, after a note from a former reader, Clayton Wickham. Park says they read more of the work and traded notes for nearly a year. He remembers the first phone call. He said "Chad!" Fore said "Owen!" Then neither of them said anything else for a while. They had exchanged dozens of emails and had not met.[1] Fore's debut novel, which expands that world, is forthcoming from Henry Holt.

Ten readers "carefully consider the thousands of submissions we receive online and by mail each year."[1] What he describes, against the fear of a void, is a reading process "as personal as it always has been—predicated on such analogue tasks as licking envelopes, exchanging notes, arguing respectfully, and making long phone calls."[1] Hard copy can now go any day of the year. He points to an FAQ for the rules. I did not fetch that page, so I am repeating the path, not a checklist of what they accept.[1]

An agent is not Friday's standard. Park already set the M.F.A. and the agent aside as requirements. The part you can use is the ask. Promise is a reason to ask for more work, not a finished object you point at a magazine because the prose was fluent. A model can produce a page that looks like promise, the way it can produce fog that looks like dread. If the generated story is the whole packet, you skipped the part where someone asks what else you have, and you have to have written it.

Do not generate the "what else" in the same sitting and call it a body of work. Ma had already sent several stories before they asked. Fore's notes ran nearly a year. The ask is a demand for more pages that can survive a person, not a demand for a longer completion. If you cannot say what else you have without asking a model to invent the rest of the life, you have a fluent page. In ["First Draft by AI, Revision by Human — A Workflow"](/blog/first-draft-by-ai-revision-by-human-a-workflow) a model can furnish a hallway. You write the next room, and the door is still in it. In ["The Integrity Framework — A Writer's Code for AI Use"](/blog/the-integrity-framework-a-writers-code-for-ai-use) the human pass is where that decision lives.

## For Next Time

Monday is ["Advanced Fiction: Multi-POV Novels"](/blog/advanced-fiction-multi-pov-novels). Four voices that are really one voice with the names changed is the same failure wearing a cast list. A persona per chapter does not leave the base-model signature just because the heading changed. The door still has to be accounted for in each head, and a paragraph that reconciles every point of view is the epiphany with a bigger table.

Homework, model closed. One door, written out: where it is, what it looks like, what it costs to use. One secret the prose knows and the other people in the scene do not. Then four hundred words that want an epiphany. Strike the sentence that announces understanding. If a reader could still tell the paint from "a run-down historical house," and the secret is still unpaid, bring the pages Monday. Do not point them at a magazine. If there is any promise in them, the next move is more of your own work.
