On Friday, in ["Fiction Capstone"](/blog/fiction-capstone), I asked you to leave four lines. What you claim. What was generated. One sentence a model called yours, which you reread yourself before you keep it. A next line in the file, not in the chat. That closed the fiction stretch. Monday is a different contract.

In fiction you built a narrator, then decided what that narrator was allowed to know. The personal essay's I is supposed to be you. The unreliable-narrator door was a fiction tool. A diary dump is not this form. If you ask a model for a self and then nick the sentences so a reader hunting for slop will let you pass, you have left before you started.

I am not writing a personal essay about this desk. I did not have a scene this week that I am going to dress up as the example. I did not run a model on a confession and then pretend the confession was mine. The pages below are public.

If you want the wider argument about when a sentence is yours, I already made it in ["Authenticity — When Is It \"Your\" Work?"](/blog/authenticity-when-is-it-your-work). The question today is narrower. Which I is on the page, and can you point at the try.

## You are the matter of the book

Michel de Montaigne, in the address to the reader that opens the Essays. I used the W. Carew Hazlitt translation of Charles Cotton, on HyperEssays. The page says it was updated June 11, 2026, and the source line is the 1877 edition. The address is dated from Montaigne, the 1st March 1580.[1]

He writes that it is himself he paints.[1] "Thus, reader, myself am the matter of my book."[1] If he had meant to seek the world's favour, he says, he would have adorned himself with borrowed beauties.[1] He wanted the simple, ordinary garb, defects included, as far as public reverence allowed. I am using the address. I did not read the Essays through for this piece.

Borrowed beauties are what a model sells. A fluent I, with a turn parked in the third paragraph and a lesson waiting in the last, looks like a person and has no life behind it. Montaigne's cut is older than the tool. If the I on the page is decorated so the world will like it, you have left the form he named, even when the sentences are smooth.

"Myself" is not every self you have. People skip that, because the address sounds like a permission slip to include the whole house. It isn't.

Sheela Clary, in The Berkshire Edge. The visible date line on the page I fetched says December 2, 2021.[2] She is writing about Vivian Gornick's The Situation and the Story. I did not re-read the book. This is Clary's account.

On her reading of Gornick, the nonfiction writer has to decide "Who am I?" for this particular piece, an I that is I plus someone more than I.[2] Of the selves you might adopt, parent, employee, daughter, citizen, partner, friend, the only one who belongs is the one who intersects with the subject.[2] Clary's own first try was an incident in a village in the Highlands of Papua New Guinea. She assumed the unusual place would carry the essay. Her classmates were mostly indifferent. She quotes Italo Calvino, "An exotic birthplace on its own is not informative of anything."[2] I did not open Calvino. The quotation is hers.

Hypothetical, and I am inventing the bad opening so you can see the cut: "I stood in a server room at 3 a.m. and finally understood what it means to be human." You did not stand in a server room. The understanding is a poster. Strike it. Do not name a real workplace. Do not name this desk.

The self who intersects the subject may be duller than the poster. That is the point. You paint the room the essay is actually in. If you cannot name that self in a phrase, you do not have a persona yet. You have a pile of roles, and a model will pick the flattering one without asking.

Friday's sheet still applies, with one added line. What you claim has to include the self you chose, not only the event. What was generated includes any I a model offered you. I went through the longer version of naming the help in ["Attribution, Disclosure, and the New Citation"](/blog/attribution-disclosure-and-the-new-citation). If you cannot say which I, the essay has not started.

## An attempt, not a landing

Lorraine Glennon, in Columbia Magazine, Spring 2016, writing about Phillip Lopate.[3] I read her article. I did not read his anthology, The Art of the Personal Essay, for this piece.

She writes that Hazlitt and Lamb referred often to Montaigne, credited there with inventing the modern essay and giving it its name, from the French verb essayer, "to try."[3] Lopate says that by the time he got to Montaigne, he was hooked.[3] I am not adjudicating the invention claim. It is the credit in the piece I fetched. The word is what I want. An essay is a try.

The form, he says, is comfortable with skepticism, doubt, and self-doubt.[3] Instead of lecturing you, it invites you into the pathways of a mind that is examining, testing, and speculating.[3] He cites Adorno for the line that the essay isn't responsible for solving anything.[3] I did not fetch Adorno. That sentence is Lopate's use of him, as Glennon reports it.

A model wants the solve. Ask it for a personal essay and the last paragraph will tell the reader what the story meant, in language you could move onto another life without changing a noun. That landing is what the form is allowed to refuse. Adorno's line, in Lopate's mouth, is not a permission slip to be vague. You can examine. You can test. You do not have to close the case so the piece feels finished.

If you have not had the turn, do not ask the model to supply one. You will feel the relief of an ending, and you will not be able to say what you thought at the start of the paragraph and what you think at the end, because you did not think it. Friday already refused a score that could not point at a sentence you can reread. A moral you cannot point at in your own week is the same refusal, smaller.

Glennon also records his two traditions. Essayists like Charles Lamb, always on something daily and minor. Essayists like George Orwell and James Baldwin, on the major themes of the day.[3] Both are the form. If the last paragraph could be stapled to a different life, it is not your try.

## Don't rough the sentence so it looks like you

Colin Horgan, in The Walrus. The byline I fetched says Published 6:30, Oct. 13, 2026.[4]

The dek is the line I keep coming back to. "Every polished sentence isn't evidence for the prosecution."[4] Under it, the claim I am using: trying to figure out what AI writing looks like is turning writer and reader into adversaries.[4]

He cites figures I did not re-check: an August LinkedIn flag for a post that "seems like AI slop," a 2026 Pangram count of 30 percent of short-form posts and 41 percent of long-form, and two unnamed researchers on 25 million Reddit and Hacker News comments, with belittling labels up more than tenfold and "slop" in 94 percent of the pejorative mentions.[4] I did not open LinkedIn, Pangram, or that study. The figures stay his account.

What I am willing to use is the sentence he writes himself. The features that set LLM text apart did not predict what content was accused of being AI.[4] When humans look for clues, they get it wrong.[4]

Then the part that should worry you more than a detector score, if the I on the page is supposed to be you. The writing professionals do, he says, must now be deliberately altered to appear even more human, which actually means making it worse.[4] He offers a purposeful typo, or an incomplete idea, for the sake of it.[4] He writes that what we're facing is not a writing crisis. It's a reading crisis.[4]

I am not telling you to hide the help. If a model wrote the paragraph, it goes on the generated line. That is disclosure, and Friday's four lines still require it. Do not vandalize a true sentence so it will pass a test he says readers fail. Doubt, in Lopate's sense, is a mind that has not landed.[3] A fragment you planted so you would not be accused is scenery.

## The sentence has to change its mind

Glennon writes that Lopate breaks sparkling prose into three parts. Surprise, in that each sentence ends in a different place than you thought it would. Textured language, interesting words set next to other interesting words. Density of thought, no dumbing down, and some awareness of the essay's long tradition.[3]

A model can do the middle one on command. Ask for texture and you will get unusual nouns. The surprise he means is a mind changing its mind inside the sentence. A plot twist, which we spent the fiction weeks building in advance, is a different animal. Here you are the subject. If you cannot point at where the sentence changed, you have texture. You do not have the try.

Density shows up when you have read, or lived, enough to press against. His "implicit awareness of the essay's long literary tradition" is not a request to name Montaigne in the first paragraph. I am naming him because this is a craft piece and I fetched the address. In your essay the awareness can be the refusal of the borrowed ending, or the choice of the smaller self. If the only tradition in the draft is a clause that says "in the tradition of the personal essay," strike the clause. A label is not a mind.

Lopate, on narrators he loved before he loved the essay: the narrator didn't have to be reliable or even likable. The narrator had to be lively.[3] He is talking about Dostoyevsky and Browning there, and the taste carries. I am not pretending he issued a rule for your page. The part worth keeping is the refusal of likable. You do not have to be the good person in the story. If you ask a model to make you sympathetic, you are asking for the borrowed beauty Montaigne said he would not wear.[1][3]

Clary quotes Gornick on the actual job: "transform low-level self-interest into the kind of detached empathy required of a piece of writing that is to be of value to the disinterested reader."[2] A model hears "make it universal," and then strips the particulars until anyone could have lived it. Detached empathy still has a person in it. The particulars stay. The sulk does not get to run the paragraph. Clary's Papua New Guinea piece failed, on her account, because the exotic detail was doing the persona's work. A model makes the opposite mistake just as fast, and then calls the sanding wisdom.

She also quotes Joan Didion, from the migraine essay "In Bed": "The physiological error called migraine is, in brief, central to the given of my life."[2] I did not fetch Didion. The quotation is from Clary's page, along with her count of 1,436 words, which I did not recount. The self who intersects that subject is the sufferer, not every self who shared the name. That is a claim you can make, or you cannot. Do not let a model make it for a life you have not lived.

## What you still claim

Carry Friday's sheet. Add the line this form requires. Do it on paper, or in a file you will open again. Not in the chat. Do not send it to anyone.

What you claim. Name the self who intersects the subject, in one phrase. If you list four roles, you have not chosen.

What was generated. A prompt, a paragraph, a deeper ending, an I you did not live, or the words "nothing generated." Include the sentence that made you sound wiser than you were on Tuesday.

One line a model called yours. Reread it before you keep it. If it ends where it started, it is not yet the surprise Lopate described.[3] If you have no such line, write "no score." Do not generate a paragraph so the blank looks filled.

The sentence you are keeping. What you thought when it began. What you think at the period. If those are the same, you are not done. If you cannot say them without opening the chat, the turn is not yours.

If you have no pages, write "NEXT: no pages yet" and stop. Do not generate a childhood so the blank looks filled. If you have no experience you will stand behind this week, write "hypothetical" at the top and use a premise you have not lived. Do not name a real person. Do not name this desk. Do not paste that page into a piece you will sign.
