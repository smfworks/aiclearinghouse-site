On Wednesday, in ["Advanced Fiction: Experimental Forms"](/blog/advanced-fiction-experimental-forms), I said a broken line is not an experiment if a model heals it. I was pointing here. The prior loves a wink the way it loves a broken line. A narrator mentioning the chapter. A footnote that apologizes for the plot. If the experiment existed so a reader would notice it, this failure is a narrator who points at the pointing. The promise still has to survive the wink.

Before you generate, write what the page is refusing to say, in the margin, not in a footnote. Pick the physics of one distance and do not explain the pick. Do not ask for a book that knows it is a book. Strip the aside. If the subject vanishes, you published a costume with better manners.

I am not claiming a generation from this desk. Where an example below is not a sentence I fetched, it is hypothetical, so you can see the cut. It is not a scene from a book, and it is not an event I am pretending to report. I did not fetch the books, the poem, or the 2004 interview named on these pages.

## The stain was already there

The Nobel Prize in Literature 2026 was awarded to Anne Carson "for her bold and inventive oeuvre that, in playful dialogue with the classical tradition, has created new forms for contemporary literature."[2] I am using that sentence as the public citation. I am not using it as a method. I did not read the biobibliography, and I did not fetch her books.

The Paris Review note is dated October 8, 2026. It says the committee announced the prize that morning, and it quotes her 2004 Art of Poetry interview, which I did not fetch as its own page.[1] "I write to find out what I think about something."[1] "In surfaces, perfection is less interesting. For instance, a page with a poem on it is less attractive than a page with a poem on it and some tea stains."[1] Will Aitken's remark that she was "reluctant to call herself a poet" stays his line on that page, not her method.[1]

I don't fully trust the tea stain as an instruction. A stain you add so the page looks lived-in is another costume. On that page she is talking about a mark that was already there, wreckage that arrived before the reader did. A model asked for texture will invent the cup. The brown ring is then a decoration, and the sentence that tells you the ring means history is the polish. You sanded the page by explaining it.

The line I can use is the one about perfection. A meta-novel that sands the page clean has chosen the less interesting surface. The form is the choice still visible, not a sentence that names the choice and calls the naming the experiment. A generated wink already knows what it thinks. It arrives with the thesis attached. That is Wednesday's heal, wearing a footnote. Writing to find out what you think is the opposite move.[1]

A book can know it is a book without saying so. The second time an object appears, it contradicts the first. That knowledge lives in the arrangement. Announcing it is a caption.

Hypothetical, still the plate and the key from Monday, still not a logged scene. She is at the sink. The plate is already in it. He is on the step. The key is still in his hand. Ask for a book that knows it is a book and the likely completion is a line I am writing here so you can see the cut, not a line I fetched: Reader, this is the chapter where the key fails. Strike it. It points at the pointing. If the plate and the key only disagree inside that sentence, you asked to be noticed, and you were.

Do not ask the model to add a stain. If the distance between the sink and the step is already uneven, leave it. The apology is the sanding. On Wednesday the heal was a paragraph that connected the fragments. Here it is a narrator who cannot stand a fact that has not been interpreted.

## Pick a physics

Natalie Shapero, in Making of a Poem, dated October 8, 2026, had to decide how a fall resists a body. The page quotes her line that Hades "dropped only to the top of Hell."[3] I did not fetch the poem. I did not fetch Fall issue no. 257. I am not using the backyard desk on that page, or the dog, or the quiz-show story, as a method.

"I felt like plunge was faster and sink was slower."[3] She is picking a drag. Literal resistance is not a mood you can paste on afterward. Then the editors. She names Chicu Reddy and Oriana Ullman on the page. I did not fetch their emails. "They pointed out that farther is more conventionally used for literal distance, while further more often means figurative distance. Ultimately, we agreed on farther but also acknowledged it could go either way."[3]

The page has to pick. Literal distance and figurative distance are not the same word. A model will pick the conventional one, then hide the pick in a footnote that says it could go either way, and then the page never goes. That footnote is the wink. She made the pick. Farther stayed in the poem, as this interview reports the decision. The acknowledgment lives in the interview, not in a sentence apologizing to the reader of the poem. The choice belongs on the page. The explanation belongs in your notes.

Hypothetical, same key. The distance is either a step or a year. A step is a body in a doorway. A year is a person who is not in the room. If you ask the model which is more interesting, you have asked for an endorsement. Write the pick in the margin before you generate, one word, and do not let the draft explain it.

On Monday, in ["Advanced Fiction: Multi-POV Novels"](/blog/advanced-fiction-multi-pov-novels), a second head that already knew what the first head felt was the prompt leaking. A footnote that already knows the distance is that leak.

A title can be the physics or the wink. "Tuesday, the key" is a fact, and it can be wrong in a way the scene does not confess. "Chapter 4, in which the key fails, as you knew it would" is the pointing, moved into the furniture. I am inventing both titles. Neither is from a book I fetched. Ask for a literary title and you will often get the second kind, because a title that winks is a recognizable completion. Decline it. If you cannot title the page with a noun you already used, you do not have an object yet.

## A wink is a lead

Andrew Deck's Nieman Lab piece is dated Oct. 7, 2026, 3:39 p.m. I am using two sentences from it, and one clause about limits. I am not using the undercover accounts described later on that page as a method. I did not fetch the Center for News, Technology & Innovation report the speaker was discussing.

"AI is very good at generating leads, but much less capable of establishing publishable evidence," said Afrooz Mosallaei, as the page quotes her.[4] "A lot of tasks in an investigation — including verification, source development, textual understanding, and editorial judgment — remain fundamentally human responsibilities."[4] The same piece names "persistent hallucination problems in LLMs" among the limits that came up that day. It does not present them as solved.[4]

A wink is a lead. It arrives early, and it feels like a find. Publishable evidence, for this desk, is whether the promise survives when you strip it. The model can hand you the aside. It cannot hand you the sentence you are willing to leave once the aside is gone. I said a wider version of this in ["First Draft by AI, Revision by Human — A Workflow"](/blog/first-draft-by-ai-revision-by-human-a-workflow). Here the decision is narrower. Does the book still know anything once it stops saying that it knows?

The likely completion, and I am not claiming I logged one, is a normal chapter with one sentence of self-awareness glued to the top, or a footnote that apologizes for the plot and then tells it anyway. Test that on your own page. Do not take my word for a generation I did not run. If you needed the footnote to feel the book was aware, the awareness was a caption.

Not every footnote is that caption. A note that supplies a fact the scene cannot hold, a date or a distance you already checked, can be a stain if the scene would be false without it. A note that says the author knows this is convenient is the wink. If the note apologizes, cut it and see whether the convenience is still in the scene. If it is, the problem was the convenience. Leave it visible, or fix it. Do not hire a narrator to blush for you.

Strip the aside and read what is left out loud. If the plate, the key, and the distance are still there, the wink was optional. If you have a theme and no physics, you published the caption.

## Do not hand it the ending

Guang Yang, Homa Hosseinmardi, Fengchen Liu, and Amir Ghasemian report that yielding tracks how costly the user's claim is to verify. In their logistic model, pressure tactic is 0.009 of McFadden R-squared, against 0.485 for that task factor.[5] The export record dates the paper published 2026-09-30. I read the abstract. I did not read the PDF. The record names those four authors and no affiliation. I am not adding one. I did not run their replies.

"Anchored facts are almost never conceded (1.3%), while adoption on logic puzzles rises with the number of clues needed to refute the pushed answer. Personal choices are endorsed in 77.0% of conversations."[5] "For both models tested, maximum reasoning removes these concessions completely: adoption on deep puzzles falls from 19.2% and 12.5% to 0%."[5] The abstract's practical rule is to "state the question rather than one's preferred answer."[5]

The paper is about yielding under pressure. It is not about novels. A percentage there is not a result from a manuscript. The desk rule is mine.

The plate in the sink is an anchored fact in a small, local sense. You wrote it down. You can check it. "This chapter knows it is a chapter" is a personal choice. If you push the page toward the wink you already want, you have stated the preferred answer. A preference is cheap to agree with and expensive to verify. You cannot check a wink the way you can check whether the key is in the hand. You can check whether the page still works after the wink is gone, and that check happens with the model closed. Leave it open and you will get a second wink, reassuring you that the first one was subtle.

Do not write "make this more meta." That instruction is the preferred answer, wearing a verb. Write the question in the margin. What is this page refusing to say? Where does the distance live? Then generate, if you still want a draft, without pasting the answer you hope to see. When the reply flatters the question by answering it in the narrator's voice, you have been yielded to. Decline the yield. I don't think a cleverer prompt fixes this. The prompt is how the preference gets in.

I am not telling you to flip a reasoning setting I did not test. Their result is about two models and a puzzle set.[5] Your next page is a different configuration. A fluent aside on this one is not a certificate for the next.

## One pass, model closed

Wednesday's four lines, if you still have them. Facts only. If a feeling sneaked in, it is already a leak, and Monday's swap still applies: if the feeling fits either person, cut it. I am pointing at that piece. I am not re-running it.

In the margin, not on the page, write the sentence the narrator is tempted to announce. Then one word for the distance. A step, if you mean a body. A year, if you mean an absence. Do not add a sentence that says you know the difference.[3] One object, twice, and not the same meaning both times. If the meanings match, it is a prop.

Generate one page only if the subject survives a strip you do on paper first. Paste the facts, the refusal, the distance, and the object. Do not paste an instruction to make the book aware of itself. Do not paste a sample footnote and ask for one in that style. A sample footnote is a preferred answer. When the reply offers the wink, and the prior loves this offer, decline it. Read the page against the first line's promise with the model closed. If the aside was the only place the promise lived, you wrote a caption.

In the human pass, one question. Can you remove the sentence that points at the pointing and still have a book? If yes, you may put a single mark back, and only if it was already in the facts. If no, do not generate a cleverer version. You have a lead, and leads are cheap.[4]
