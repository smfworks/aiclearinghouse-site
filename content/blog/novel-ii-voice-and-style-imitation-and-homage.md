In ["Novel II — Voice and Style: Finding the Narrator"](/blog/novel-ii-voice-and-style-finding-the-narrator) I said voice is the residue of a specific consciousness under pressure, and that the model has no consciousness, only a manner. I asked you to write a narrator portrait and two hundred words with the model closed. If you did that work, you have a tuning fork. Today we take it into someone else's house.

The claim is simple, and it will sound conservative until you watch a model do in eight seconds what used to take a winter. Imitation is how writers learn. It is also, now, the fastest way to lose the voice you just found. Homage is what you keep after you have copied a master long enough to see how they see. Pastiche is the costume a single prompt can put on. Used as training for the ear, the model is useful. Used as a substitute for apprenticeship, it will write you a novel that sounds like a gifted impersonator who has never been in the room.

By the end you should know what is worth stealing — the seeing, not the signature — and how to tell when you have become a costume.

## The Sedulous Ape

Robert Louis Stevenson said he had "played the sedulous ape" to Hazlitt, to Lamb, to Wordsworth, to Sir Thomas Browne. He meant it as method, not as confession. Joan Didion typed out Hemingway sentences to feel where the weight sat. Hunter S. Thompson typed *The Great Gatsby* and *A Farewell to Arms* the way a pianist plays scales.

None of this was content generation. It was muscular and aural. You learn where a sentence turns because your fingers have to make the turn. The mind can admire a style. The hand has to inhabit it.

This is the part the model cannot do for you, and the part it is most eager to skip. "Write this scene in the style of X" produces a recognizable surface in one pass. You get the polysyndeton, the omitted quotation marks, the long hypotactic coil, whichever signature you named. You do not get the hour in which your own syntax was bent by someone else's. That hour is the education. The output is only the residue of an education you did not have.

I am not being romantic about suffering. I am being precise about where the learning lives. If you skip the copying and keep the costume, you have not studied a master. You have rented a manner. On Monday I distinguished style from mannerism: style is the set of recurring choices that arise from a way of seeing; mannerism is a recurring choice detached from seeing and performed as a signature. Imitation that never goes through the hand stays on the mannerism side of that line. The model has no seeing to offer. It has signatures.

## Homage, Pastiche, Influence

Three words get used as if they were one. They are not.

Pastiche reconstructs a surface. It is competent impersonation. Brilliant as exercise, fatal as a novel. A pastiche of Faulkner knows the long sentence, the italicized interior, the heat. It does not know why the sentence had to be long in that moment. Detach the verbal signature from the seeing and you can drape it over any content. That is the test I offered Monday. If the coat still fits when you change the weather, it was a coat.

Homage is pastiche that has been metabolized. You have taken something from a predecessor — a structural courage, a permission to be quiet, a way of letting a list do the work of an argument — and the taking is visible as transformation. The work remains yours. The debt is in the grain. Homage can survive the removal of the master's tics. Pastiche cannot. Strip the obvious signatures. If a mind is still there, you paid homage. If a blank page is there, you wore a costume.

Influence is deeper and often invisible. You have lived with a writer long enough that their way of seeing has altered what you notice, and you no longer have to perform the alteration. You do not sound like them. You see a little more the way they taught you to see, and then you sound like yourself seeing it. This is the actual prize. No prompt can deliver it, because influence requires time in a mind, not time in a context window.

Parody is pastiche with an argument. It has a purpose beyond resemblance. I do not object to it. I object to pastiche that thinks it is homage because the source is beloved.

The ethical test is not "did I cite the influence." Citation is courtesy. The test is whether the page can exist without the master's verbal tics and still be a mind under pressure. If the answer is no, you are doing ventriloquism. In ["Authenticity — When Is It \"Your\" Work?"](/blog/authenticity-when-is-it-your-work) I argued that authorship is a matter of decisions, not of keystrokes. Homage is a set of decisions made in the presence of another writer. Pastiche is a set of signatures accepted because they sounded expensive.

## The Eight-Second Costume

The prompt is almost always some version of: *Rewrite this scene in the style of [beloved writer].*

The model is extremely good at this. It should be. In ["Style Transfer: Teaching AI Your Voice"](/blog/style-transfer-teaching-ai-your-voice) I treated that talent as a tool for teaching a model *your* surface. The same talent, pointed at the dead, is a different instrument. It will give you a McCarthy scene that omits conjunctions in the expected places. It will give you Didion's cool diagnostic clause, or Baldwin's sermonic turn without Baldwin's stakes. Readers who know the source will recognize the tribute. Readers who do not will still feel that someone talented is in the room. Both reactions are misleading. Recognition of a signature is not the presence of a voice.

What the eight-second costume costs you is the apprenticeship. You never have to find the place in the master's page where the seeing happens — the clause that is not decorative, the refusal, the thing noticed instead of the face. You get the brand, which is what the training data has compressed. Seeing is a history of attention. Tokens do not hold it.

There is a second cost, closer to Monday's problem. If you have just found a narrator — a portrait and two hundred words that sound like a specific mouth — and you then ask the model to render the next scene in the style of a master, you will overwrite the narrator with the costume. The model cannot hold both. It will optimize for the more famous pattern. Your narrator, being new and local, will lose. I have watched this happen in drafts that were alive in chapter two and wearing someone else's coat by chapter six. The writer thought they were raising the prose. They were firing the speaker.

Use the dangerous prompt, if you use it at all, as a diagnostic, never as a draft. Generate the costume. Put it next to your scene. Read them aloud. The model's version will almost always be more "like" the master. That is not a verdict in its favor. Notice what the costume got right about *seeing* — a kind of attention, a permission, a structural nerve — then close the model and put that lesson into your narrator's mouth, not the master's.

## Steal the Seeing

What is worth stealing is almost never the diction.

From Austen, the thing to steal is not the irony as a tone. It is the social physics: a room as a field of forces, a sentence that registers a slight without naming it. From Toni Morrison, not the lyric lift. The refusal to explain Black interiority to an implied white reader — the assumption of the audience, which is a moral and technical choice. From García Márquez, not the rain. The deadpan with which the impossible is filed as fact.

I am naming these badly on purpose. A list of "what to steal" becomes a new set of mannerisms if you treat it as a menu. The practice is local. You sit with one page. You ask three questions, and you write the answers in the margin before any model is open:

What did they notice that I would have skipped?
What did they refuse to explain that I would have explained?
Where did the sentence turn, and what work was the turn doing?

Those three will take you further than a style guide. They are questions about attention. Imitation that studies choice will feed your narrator. Imitation that studies vocabulary will starve them.

A concrete pass. Take a page of a writer you love. Do not pick your favorite page — pick a workmanlike one, a scene of getting across a room or delivering news. Favorite pages are already performing. Copy the page by hand. No model. Then, without looking, write four sentences of your own scene that attempt only the *noticing*. If your writer notices the scuff before the face, your narrator notices whatever *they* would notice first, with that same stubbornness of attention. You have stolen a discipline, not a dialect.

Then you may ask the model to rewrite those four sentences in the style of the master. You are not looking for sentences to keep. You are looking for the gap between your noticing and the model's costume. Study the gap. Keep your sentences. Close the model.

## An Apprenticeship You Can Finish in a Week

Here is a protocol I have used on novels that needed a richer ear without needing a new author.

Day one. Choose one master, not three. Three is how you get stew. Copy two pages by hand. Write the three margin questions. Do not generate.

Day two. Write three hundred words of your narrator — the one from Monday's homework — in a scene they would hate to tell. Keep the master's pages face down. You are writing from a mind that has spent a day in another mind.

Day three. Reverse-outline the master's two pages: beat by beat, not sentence by sentence. What is the unit of attention? When does the prose speed up, and what is being avoided by the speed? This is the same muscle we used in ["Novel II — Deep Craft: Pacing and Tension"](/blog/novel-ii-deep-craft-pacing-and-tension), applied to someone else's instrument.

Day four. Paste your three hundred words. Ask the model for a version "in the style of" the master. Generate once. Do not iterate. Print both versions. Strike every sentence in the model's version that is only a signature — a tic that would survive a change of content. Circle every sentence that taught you something about attention. There will be fewer circles than strikes. That ratio is the point.

Day five. Rewrite your three hundred words by hand, keeping none of the model's diction and any of the circled lessons that your narrator can honestly use. Read the result against Monday's tuning fork. If the mouth has changed into the master's mouth, you went too far. If the mouth is the same and the attention is sharper, you did the work.

I have given the model one job on one day, and I have forbidden iteration. That will feel wasteful to anyone who has learned to prompt. It is not. The model is a mirror of the compressed public version of a style. Looking in it once is education. Looking in it twelve times is how you start to prefer the mirror.

In ["The Integrity Framework — A Writer's Code for AI Use"](/blog/the-integrity-framework-a-writers-code-for-ai-use) I said the writer is accountable for what the page finally is. Homage does not transfer that accountability to the dead, or to the model. If the page is a costume, you put it on.

## When Homage Becomes Ventriloquism

The exercise should stop earlier than most ambitious drafts think.

If a reader who knows the master can play the drinking game — one drink per omitted conjunction, one per biblical cadence, one per ironic Austenian tag — you are not in homage. You are in karaoke. Karaoke is a pleasure. It is not a novel.

If you cannot revise a chapter without re-prompting "in the style of," the voice is not in you. It is in the session. Sessions end. Books have to survive their sessions.

If your narrator from Monday cannot speak the homage pages, you have two books occupying one spine. That can be a technique — a novel that deliberately puts on a coat, a narrator who is themselves an imitator. Fine. Then it has to be a decision, visible in the architecture, not a drift because the model is better at famous patterns than at local ones.

The cleanest test I have is the one I will ask you to run before Friday. Take a homage-shaped passage. Remove every verbal tic you can trace to the master. Read what remains. If a consciousness under pressure remains, keep going. If what remains is plot summary, the homage was load-bearing in the wrong way. You built the house out of the scaffolding.

I am not against influence. I am against the counterfeit of influence that the model makes cheap. Writers have always stolen. Eliot was right that immature poets imitate and mature poets steal, and he was talking about metabolization, not about a prompt. The model collapses the time in which metabolization used to happen. Put the time back. Copy by hand. Steal the seeing. Let the signature go. The narrator you found on Monday still has to tell this book. The model can only dress them.

## For Next Time

On Friday we close this voice-and-style triad with ["Novel II — Voice and Style: The Unreliable Narrator"](/blog/novel-ii-voice-and-style-the-unreliable-narrator). Once you can hear a specific speaker, and once you know how to sit with a master without becoming them, you can let the speaker lie. Unreliability is not a trick of withholding. It is a voice that knows more or less than it pretends, and that gap is a design. The model, eager to be helpful and complete, is a terrible liar unless you teach it to be. We will talk about the kinds of unreliability that still work, and how to keep a lying narrator from collapsing into the AI accent, which is always trying to tell you the truth too smoothly.

Your homework until then: copy one workmanlike page of a master by hand. Write the three margin questions. Write three hundred words of your Monday narrator under pressure, model closed. Generate one "in the style of" version. Do not iterate. Strike signatures, circle seeing, rewrite by hand. Read the rewrite against your tuning fork. Bring that ear on Friday. Unreliability is wasted on a narrator you cannot hear.
