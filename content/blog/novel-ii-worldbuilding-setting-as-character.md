In ["Novel II — Voice and Style: The Unreliable Narrator"](/blog/novel-ii-voice-and-style-the-unreliable-narrator) I said unreliability is a designed gap between what a speaker pretends and what the page lets a careful reader reconstruct. I asked you to keep the truth document out of the prompt, and to bring a mouth you could hear. If you did that work, you have a speaker. Today we give that speaker a world that will not automatically take their side.

The claim is older than the workshop slogan it sounds like. Setting is not the paragraph you write after the people have spoken. It is a set of pressures that make some speech possible, some speech expensive, and some speech a lie. A place that only looks is wallpaper. A place that wants something — silence, water, a particular kind of visitor, the continuation of a story the town has already agreed on — is a character. The model, trained on travel writing, listing copy, and a century of scene-setting that was already decorative, will give you wallpaper at industrial scale. It will call the wallpaper atmosphere.

By the end you should be able to name what your place wants, keep the brochure out of the scene, and tell a second witness from a backdrop.

## Wallpaper Is Not a World

Most drafts I see, AI-assisted or not, treat setting as inventory. The room has a window. The window has rain. The rain is "sleeting" or "needling" or whichever adjective the model reached for that day. None of this is false. None of it is doing character work. Description answers *what is here*. Setting answers *what does here do to them*.

Shirley Jackson's Hill House is not a floor plan. It is a will. 124 Bluestone Road in *Beloved* is not an address. It is a house that is "spiteful," then "loud," then "quiet," and each change is a plot event, not a renovation. Darlington Hall in *The Remains of the Day* is the professional language Stevens cannot leave. The house does not have to speak. It has already written his sentences.

When I say setting as character I do not mean a talking castle, unless you are writing a talking castle. I mean the place has desire, method, and cost, the same three things I would demand of a person. Desire: what the place is trying to keep or become. Method: how it acts on bodies and speech. Cost: what it takes from anyone who stays. If you cannot fill those three, you have scenery. Scenery is cheap. The model will generate it forever.

In ["Novel II — Deep Craft: Showing vs Telling"](/blog/novel-ii-deep-craft-showing-vs-telling) I said showing is not more adjectives. It is evidence the reader can work. A setting that acts is evidence. A setting that is listed is a caption.

## The Brochure Problem

Here is the AI accent of worldbuilding, and it is more specific than the accent I named when we talked about voice.

Ask a model to establish a town, a ship, a station, a house, and you will usually get a brochure. The streets "hum with life." The market is "a riot of color and sound." The mountains are "majestic." History is "rich." Someone has lived here for generations, unnamed. The light is always doing something interesting. This is not hallucination in the factual sense. It is a genre the training data loves: the paragraph whose job is to prove that a place exists, written for a reader who is shopping.

Travel copy has a client. The client wants to go. Fiction has a different client. The reader wants to know what the place will do to the people they have agreed to care about. Those are opposite assignments. The brochure makes the place available. The novel makes the place consequential.

The second substitute is the lore dump. You asked for a world, so the model, being helpful and complete, delivers the founding, the three ruling families, the mineral that makes the economy possible, and a proverb. All of that may be true in your bible. Almost none of it belongs in the scene. On Friday I said the model smuggles truth because it has been rewarded for making the hidden thing available. It smuggles encyclopedia the same way. Completeness is a virtue in an assistant. It is death on an opening page.

The third substitute is weather as mood. Rain for sadness. Heat for tension. Fog for confusion. I am not against weather. I am against weather that has not been given a job beyond the emotion you already decided the scene should have. If the rain does not change what a character can afford to say, it is wallpaper with a soundtrack.

Strike the brochure on sight. If a sentence would sit comfortably in a tourism board's copy, it cannot stay, no matter how pretty the cadence. Pretty is the model's first offering. Consequence is yours.

## A Second Witness

Friday I said a lying narrator needs a world that does not collude. This is the technical reason setting has to be a character, not a backdrop.

A backdrop agrees with whoever is speaking. The house is "warm" because the memoirist needs it to have been warm. The town is "close-knit" because the returning daughter needs to believe she was held. The model, matching the narrator's diction, will collude by default. It will make the world an accessory of the voice. That is a failure of unreliability and a failure of setting at the same time. You have one mind on the page, wearing a landscape.

A second witness does not have to contradict in neon. Hill House does not stand up and call Eleanor a liar. It fails to match her hope. 124 does not footnote Sethe's account. It behaves. Darlington Hall does not announce that Stevens has wasted his life. It keeps offering him silver to polish. The friction is the reader's to feel, the same way the gap in an unreliable narrator is the reader's to reconstruct.

This is why you do not put the world's verdict in the prompt either. If you tell the model "the house is colder than she admits," it will write the temperature with a wink. You have seen this wink. *The radiator ticked, though she would have said the room was warm.* That is the implied author climbing into the furniture. Setting as character means the radiator ticks, and she says the room is warm, and you do not write the "though."

In ["Authenticity — When Is It \"Your\" Work?"](/blog/authenticity-when-is-it-your-work) I argued that authorship is a matter of decisions. The decision here is this: the place is allowed to be wrong about the narrator, and the narrator is allowed to be wrong about the place, and neither of them gets to editorialize the disagreement.

Yoknapatawpha is a second witness that lasts a career. Gatsby's West Egg is a second witness that lasts a summer. You do not need Faulkner's county. You need a place that can disagree.

## Give the Place a Want

Do this on paper, model closed, before any scene.

Name the place the way you named the narrator in ["Finding the Narrator"](/blog/novel-ii-voice-and-style-finding-the-narrator): a portrait, not a map. One page. Three headings.

Want: what this place is trying to keep or become. A drought wants the well unused by strangers. A company town wants the accident unspoken. A boarding school wants the children to believe they were chosen. A coastal village wants the sea to remain a living, not a lawsuit. Be specific enough that another town in the same biome would want something else.

Method: how the want reaches a body. Not "oppressive atmosphere." The shift whistle. The way windows face the mill, not the river. The path that goes around the old house. The word no one uses for the mine. Method is procedural. If you cannot point to a repeated action, you do not have a method. You have a vibe, and vibes are what the model is for.

Cost: what staying takes. Dignity, a language, a son, the habit of looking up. Cost has to be payable in the plot, not only in the theme. If the cost never appears as a choice a character makes, it is a caption.

Then write one paragraph in which the place acts and no one describes it. The mill whistle, not "the industrial town." The unwatered lawn, not "a neighborhood in decline." If you cannot do this paragraph without adjectives of mood, the want is still an idea. Ideas do not pressure speech. Procedures do.

This is the same discipline as the narrator portrait. You are not decorating. You are hiring a second consciousness that does not get dialogue tags.

## Two Documents, Again

The practical method will look familiar, because the failure mode is the same.

Document W is the world. Want, method, cost. The rules that do not change because a scene is sad. Who owns the water. What the hill does to sound. Which door no one uses. You write this yourself. It can be a page. It can be ten, if you are building a planet. It is not a chapter. It is a map of pressure. Do not paste it into a scene prompt. If you do, the model will smuggle it. You will get a character thinking, helpfully, about the class structure of the harbor, in language no one at the harbor would use.

Document S is the scene. Who is in the room, what they want in the next four hundred words, what they will not say, and two or three physical constraints from Document W — not the lore, the constraints. The well is low. The whistle will go at six. The window faces the mill. That is enough. The model can work with a dry well. It cannot work with your anthropology, and it will pretend it can.

Generate from Document S. Read for brochure, for lore leak, for weather-as-mood, and for collusion: places where the setting has agreed with the narrator too quickly. Mark them. Rewrite by hand, with Document W open next to you and the model closed. The rewrite is the book. The generation is a sketch of furniture.

In ["First Draft by AI, Revision by Human — A Workflow"](/blog/first-draft-by-ai-revision-by-human-a-workflow) I said the human pass is where decisions live. Setting-as-character is a decision you cannot outsource, because the model has no stake in the place. It has never been thirsty there.

If you need the model as a brainstorming partner for Document W, use it that way, then lock the page. Brainstorming is not drafting. Once the want is chosen, stop asking the model what the town is like. You know. Asking again is how the brochure gets back in.

## Pressure, Not Inventory

A test I use in line edits: delete every sentence whose only job is to prove the place exists. Read what remains. If the scene still has weather, architecture, and objects, but no one is being changed by them, you deleted inventory and found a conversation in a white room. That is useful. It means the setting was never in the scene. It was in the captions.

Put one pressure back. Not three. One. The whistle, the low well, the door. Let it interfere with a line of dialogue, a piece of blocking, a decision to stay or leave. If the pressure does not change a line, it is still inventory. Try a different pressure.

This is how setting becomes character without a talking house. Character, in the sense I am using, is a pattern of interference. Your narrator interferes with the truth. Your setting interferes with the narrator. The reader watches two patterns miss each other, and that miss is the book.

Do not ask the model for "more atmosphere." Atmosphere is what you have when you have not chosen a pressure. Ask yourself, on paper: what does this place prevent them from doing in the next two minutes. Write that. The rest of the furniture can wait. Most of it can wait forever.

West Egg does not need another description of the bay. It needs the green light to keep costing Gatsby the evening. Hailsham, in *Never Let Me Go*, does not need a prospectus. It needs the rumor of the gallery to keep the children obedient. Arrakis does not need a climate appendix in the scene. It needs the stillsuit, and what the stillsuit does to a conversation held in open air. Steal the interference, not the postcard.

In ["Novel II — Deep Craft: Pacing and Tension"](/blog/novel-ii-deep-craft-pacing-and-tension) I said tension is the reader's desire to know. A second witness feeds that desire without a new secret. The reader already knows the narrator is speaking. Now they want to know whether the house believes them.

## For Next Time

On Wednesday we stay in the same town, or planet, or house, and refuse to let it drift: ["Novel II — Worldbuilding: Consistency Engines"](/blog/novel-ii-worldbuilding-consistency-engines). A place that wants something on page twelve has to keep wanting it on page three hundred, or the want was a mood. The model, which does not remember a well the way a body remembers thirst, will happily refill the aquifer because the scene is now a love scene and love scenes, in the training data, happen near water. Consistency is not pedantry. It is how a second witness stays a character instead of reverting to wallpaper. We will talk about the ledgers that catch the refill, and the prompts that cause it.

Your homework until then: take Friday's narrator and this week's place. Write Document W, one page, model closed: want, method, cost. Then write four hundred words in which the place acts on the narrator's lie — the house colder than the memoir, the town that does not match the legend — still model closed. Generate the same scene once from a scene prompt that contains constraints, never the bible. Mark brochure, leaks, winks, and collusion. Strike them. Rewrite by hand. If the place could be swapped for another place without the lie changing, you wrote wallpaper. If the lie has to work harder because of where it is being told, you hired a second witness. Bring that friction on Wednesday. A consistency engine is wasted on a world that does not yet want anything.
