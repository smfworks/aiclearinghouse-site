On Friday, in ["Genre: Literary Fiction"](/blog/genre-literary-fiction), I said four voices that are really one voice with the names changed is the same failure wearing a cast list. A persona typed at the top of a chapter does not make a second person. The paragraph that sits everyone down and explains what they all meant is the epiphany with a bigger table.

In ["Novel II — Deep Craft: Point of View"](/blog/novel-ii-deep-craft-point-of-view) I treated point of view as a contract, not a camera angle. That contract still holds when you add a head. What changes is how cheaply a model can counterfeit it. The model keeps the event. It changes the name. It calls the result a novel with more than one point of view.

By the end you should be able to split three things the model will try to share: the first feeling, the office each person wants from the other, and the direction each head is facing. If those three can be swapped between chapters without a reader noticing, you do not have two people. You have one contract filed under several names.

I am not claiming a generation from this desk. Where an example below is not a sentence I fetched, it is hypothetical, so you can see the cut. It is not a scene from a book, and it is not an event I am pretending to report.

## The sting both heads already feel

Katherine Reay, in a Writer's Digest piece on emotions that feel real, starts where a model starts. "I say we start with the obvious. Then reject it."[1]

She is talking about a betrayal between two women. The page does not name the friend whose novel occasioned the piece, and I am not naming her. I did not fetch that book.[1] Readers already feel the sting. The author who stays on it is repeating a feeling the reader carried into the room. The opening is the chance to do something else. "We have the opportunity to fill out both women’s emotional landscapes and bring unexpected and multi-faceted responses to the page, emotions that deepen the reader’s connection and surprise them."[1]

"Emotional landscapes" is her wording. I am not adopting it. The instruction I can use is narrower. "What about regret? Or guilt? Not on the perpetrator’s side, but on the victim’s."[1] Then: "Mine the third, fourth, or even fifth reactions that come to mind rather than the first or second."[1]

A generated pair of chapters will not mine. It will complete. You hand it the event and ask for her chapter, then his. Both heads receive the sting, because the sting is the likely next sentence. Hurt in the one who was crossed. Shame, or a justification that still orbits the shame, in the one who did the crossing. You asked for two points of view. You received the reader's first feeling, typed twice, with the names swapped.

That is the failure Friday named, moved from theme to feeling. On Friday the prior finished the scene by announcing what it meant. Here it finishes the scene by announcing what everyone felt, and what everyone felt is the obvious thing.

Here is the cut, hypothetical, not a logged generation and not a sentence from Reay's page. Shared facts only. She told him the account was empty. It was not empty. The number on the paper is the same number in both chapters. If you take the first reaction, his chapter is the heat in his face. Her chapter is the fear of being caught. Anyone who has watched a fight on a screen can write those two. They are the audience, not the people.

His third reaction might be relief. The lie is now a thing in the room, and he does not have to keep pretending he believes the paper. Her third reaction might not be fear. It might be a mean pride that she kept the number off the page for six months, and that he only noticed because she decided to let him. If you can swap the two reactions and both chapters still read, you picked from the pile.

Write the third reaction on paper before you generate either chapter. If you cannot name it without asking the model what someone in that situation would feel, you are asking for the first reaction and hoping a later number will disguise it. The model will not reject the obvious feeling for you. Rejection is the human pass.

I don't trust a second chapter that already knows what the first chapter felt. That knowledge is usually the prompt leaking, not the character perceiving. If he was not in the room, and he has not been told, his chapter does not get her heat. Facts cross when you let them cross. Feelings do not get a group chat.

## The office, not the mask

Kayla Hardy, writing in Writer's Digest about *The Quarter Queen*, will not let one mask do the work of two people. I did not fetch the novel. I am using the essay, and I am not adding a claim past it.

She wanted to see Marie Laveau "as a powerful contradiction of forces in one cryptic woman, to see her as both mother and daughter, hero and villain, woman and witch."[2] That list is one figure carrying more than one force. The split I can use is between two people who want different things from the same bond. "In Ree we find a young woman who just truly and desperately wants her mother to be her mother."[2] "Tragically, this is something Marie Laveau can never truly ever be because the morally gray world she inhabits demands she always will be The Quarter Queen first."[2]

Hardy writes that Marie Laveau II, the real-life daughter and successor to Marie Laveau I, is known in the book as Marie "Ree" Laveau the Second.[2] The daughter wants a mother. The mother is the role first. They share a relationship. They do not share an office.

A model told to write her chapter, then his, will hand both of them the same mask: conflicted, loving, tired of the secrets. You can feel the care in the sentences and still have one person. The care is the mask. The office is the disagreement about what the relationship is for.

In ["The Multi-Voice Workshop — Switching Personas Mid-Project"](/blog/the-multi-voice-workshop-switching-personas-mid-project) I said a writer can move between stances on purpose. That is still true of a person at a desk. It is a bad description of a chapter break. A new heading does not leave the voice that just wrote the mother. On Friday I said a persona per chapter does not walk you out of the base model's signature because the heading changed. If both chapters want the same office, the heading was a costume.

Before you generate the second chapter, write one line for each head. The office they want the other person to hold. The office the situation is actually requiring. If the lines match, stop. You are about to write the same chapter twice.

Hypothetical again, so the test has a shape. She wants him to be the person who comes home and eats what she left. He wants the house to stay a place he can enter without filing a report. Those are not two moods of one want. A generated pair will sand them into a single clause: they both want to be understood. If your summary of both chapters fits in that clause, you have not split anyone. You have written a theme and given it two names.

## The door this head is facing

Sophia Huneycutt, in a Writer's Digest interview by Robert Lee Brewer, describes an error her copyeditors caught that has nothing to do with theme and everything to do with a body in a room. "Most interestingly, my copyeditors helped with my characters’ logistics. For example, before their help, a character would back up a few steps into a door that they’d been facing seconds ago."[3]

I did not fetch *The House Built on Alligator Bones*. The interview lists Dutton Books / Penguin Random House as the publisher and October 6, 2026 as the release date.[3] I am not reviewing the book. A date on a page is not a reason to pretend I have read it. I am using the door.

On Friday the door had to be accounted for. Where it is. What it looks like. What it costs to use. In a novel told from more than one point of view, the door also has a facing, and the facing belongs to the head who is about to move. A model writing chapter two will reuse chapter one's facing and call the reuse continuity. Continuity of the furniture is not continuity of the person. Your second head will back into a feeling the first head was facing, because the last chapter is still in the context, and the prior would rather smooth a contradiction than keep it.

Write the facing before the chapter. Which way is this person turned. What is behind them. What would it cost, in steps, to use the door the other chapter just used. If you cannot answer that without generating, you do not have a second point of view yet.

Let the frame arrive late. Huneycutt says the frame she kept "came to me in one of the final stages of editing."[3] She had just emphasized two competing desires: "her desperate need for family and her desire to be a “good” person."[3] The structure that could hold those wants showed up after the wants were on the page. She did not lock the chapter order while the people were still a premise.

If you generate the whole braid in one sitting, you lock the frame while the heads are still wearing the same mask. Later, when you find the third reaction, the order will fight you, because every chapter was written facing the door the outline was facing. Do not ask the model to propose that braid first. Write until the offices disagree on paper. If the only order that seems to work ends with someone explaining the others, you have Friday's refund. The lights came on so the reader would not have to carry two facts at once.

Put the door into that coming-home sketch. Still hypothetical. She is at the sink, facing the kitchen door, plate already in it. He is on the step, facing the street, key still in his hand. Generate his chapter after hers and the model will have him notice the plate and feel her waiting, because her facing leaked. He does not get that feeling until he turns. The chapter where he comes in already sorry is the first reaction. The step, and which way he is facing when he takes it, is the person.

## One pass, model closed

Write the event in four lines. Shared facts only. Time, place, the object, what was said or not said. Nobody feels anything in these lines. If a feeling sneaks in, you already picked a head and called it the world. Strike it. Leave the plate, the key, the number, the hour.

For each point of view, write the third reaction, not the first. If it is the sting both people would already feel, reject it and write the next one.[1] Two heads are enough to see the failure. Four heads with the same sting are a longer filing error, not a richer book.

For each head, write the office they want and the office the situation requires. If both lines collapse to "they want to be seen," you have not split the office.[2] Seen by whom, as what, at what cost to the other role.

For each head, write the facing, including the door. Where they are turned. What is behind them. Which object they cannot yet see.[3] If chapter two can see everything chapter one saw, you changed the caption, not the head.

Only then, if you still want a draft, generate one chapter at a time, with the other chapters out of the prompt. Paste the four lines, that head's third reaction, that head's office, that head's facing. Do not paste the other head's interiority and call it continuity. Continuity of fact is the four lines. Continuity of feeling is how the names stay and the person doesn't.

Read the chapters side by side and try the swap. Move a paragraph of interiority from one head to the other. If it fits, it does not belong to either of them yet. Cut it, or rewrite it until the swap fails. Do not ask a second model whether the voices are distinct. On Friday, agreement across models was not a workshop. It was one signature, counted more than once. I am pointing at that piece. I am not re-running a measurement.

In ["First Draft by AI, Revision by Human — A Workflow"](/blog/first-draft-by-ai-revision-by-human-a-workflow) the human pass is where the decision lives. Here the decision is whether these are two people. A fluent pair can still fail the swap. Fluency is how the failure hides.

People do want similar things. Keep a similarity that survives the rejection of the obvious feeling. The failure is the similar want that arrived because it was the likely sentence. If every head knows what every other head feels, you wrote an omniscient narrator who got tired and started using names. I have not read Reay's friend's novel, Hardy's novel, or Huneycutt's for this piece. The essays are the sources. Those pages link the books. I am not sending you there to borrow a plot.

## For next time

Wednesday is ["Advanced Fiction: Experimental Forms"](/blog/advanced-fiction-experimental-forms). The prior loves a form it can point at: a broken line, a second column, a chapter shaped like an index. If the experiment exists so a reader will notice the experiment, you have the cast list problem again, this time in the typography. The third reaction still has to belong to someone. A form that falls apart when you read it as sentences was locked before the people disagreed.

Homework, model closed. One event, four lines, no feelings in them. Two heads. For each head, the third reaction, the office they want, and which way they are facing, including the door. Try to swap the reactions. If the swap works, you are not ready to generate. If it fails, you may have two people. Do not write the chapter in which someone explains them to each other. Bring the lines Wednesday.
